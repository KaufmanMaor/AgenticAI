## 1. US residential market size, commissions, representation, Austin/Texas

### Takeaway
- **Volume is stuck.** US existing-home sales were about 4.06M in both 2024 and 2025, a 30-year low. NAR's forecast of a 14% rebound in 2026 has not shown up: the mid-2026 pace is still about 4.06M SAAR.
- **Commissions survived the settlement.** After the 2024 NAR settlement, buyer-agent commissions dipped and then recovered to about 2.4%. Combined commissions are about 5.4%. Most sellers still pay the buyer's agent.
- **FSBO is near zero.** It hit a record low of 5%. About 88-91% of buyers and sellers use an agent, so the "self-represented" segment is small.
- **Texas adds a written-agreement rule.** Since 2026-01-01, Texas law requires a written buyer agreement (representation, or a 14-day non-representation showing agreement) before a showing.
- **Austin is soft but drifting toward balance.** Metro inventory is 4.4-6.5 months, median prices are about flat, concessions are common, and about 25-30% of listings have had a price cut.

## 2. Where negotiation actually happens (Texas deal flow) and failure rates

### Takeaway
A Texas deal has at least four separate negotiation rounds, so the idea of "agent-to-agent bargaining over repairs, credits and terms" matches how deals really work:
1. **Offer:** price, closing costs, earnest money, option fee and option days.
2. **Option period:** inspection, then a repair or credit amendment.
3. **Appraisal gap:** handled through the financing or appraisal addenda.
4. **Pre-close issues:** survey, title, and closing date.

The Texas option period gives the buyer an unrestricted right to terminate. That makes the post-inspection renegotiation the single highest-leverage moment, and it is also a main reason Texas leads the US in cancellations.

Concessions are now the norm. About 44-46% of US sales include them, and three-quarters do in some Sun Belt metros. Cancellations run about 14-15% nationally and 17-22% in several Texas metros. Redfin agents attribute about 70% of cancellations to inspection or repair issues. Even recovering a modest share of these failed deals would be valuable.

Typical values are $100-$500 (some sources say $250-$1,000) and 5-10 days in Austin in 2026. The fee goes to escrow within about 3 days of the effective date. Sources disagree on which form version is current (20-18 vs 20-19). ## 3. Licensing law: who may negotiate, and the Texas intermediary rules

### Takeaway
- **In Texas, negotiating is brokerage.** Anyone who negotiates or attempts to negotiate the sale or purchase of real estate for another person, for compensation or the expectation of it, is a "broker" and needs a TREC license. Business entities need one too. Unlicensed brokerage is a Class A misdemeanor (up to 1 year in jail and a $4,000 fine), and licensed brokers cannot pay unlicensed people for broker acts. Texas-licensed attorneys and principals acting for themselves are outside the Act.
- **An unlicensed AI company negotiating for a fee is high-risk.** That includes a success fee, a flat fee, or a subscription tied to the deal.
- **Regulators are treating AI as the broker's responsibility.** California DRE (Mar 2026) and Oklahoma OREC say AI tools doing licensed activity are like unlicensed assistants, and the broker answers for them. NAR's model policy (Mar 2026) says agentic AI "may not perform activities that require a real estate license."
- **There are three viable structures:**
  - **(a)** A licensed Texas brokerage (a broker of record plus agents) that uses AI and has a human license holder accountable for each negotiation. This is the Homa/locqube model.
  - **(b)** B2B software sold to licensed agents and brokerages, where the human agent stays the negotiator of record.
  - **(c)** Tooling for self-represented principals (FSBO sellers, unrepresented buyers) that coaches and drafts but does not act "for another." The risk is lower, but the segment is small (5% FSBO; about 9-12% of buyers unrepresented), and the "negotiates for another" line gets blurry if the AI talks to the counterparty directly.
- **Same-brokerage "agent vs agent" is legally an intermediary deal.** If both sides' AI agents run under the same Texas brokerage, the transaction is an intermediary relationship. That needs written consent from both parties, appointed license holders per side, and strict confidentiality: for example, no telling the buyer the seller would take less without written authorization. An architecture where one company's two agents negotiate against each other has to enforce those information walls, and both principals must consent.

### Cited Findings
- §1101.002(1) defines a broker as a person who, "in exchange for a commission or other valuable consideration or with the expectation of receiving" it, performs for another person acts including "negotiates or attempts to negotiate the listing, sale, exchange, purchase, or lease of real estate." This wording comes from search excerpts and should be confirmed against the official text. [Texas Legislature, OC ch. 1101](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/OC/htm/OC.1101.htm), [texas.public.law §1101.002](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._occ._code_section_1101.002)
- §1101.351: no person or business entity may act as a broker or sales agent without a license. [texas.public.law §1101.351](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._occ._code_section_1101.351)
- Unlicensed activity is a Class A misdemeanor, up to 1 year in county jail and a fine up to $4,000. [TREC FAQ](https://www.trec.texas.gov/what-are-penalties-unlicensed-brokerage-activity)
- §1101.651: a broker may not pay compensation for broker acts to an unlicensed person. [texas.public.law §1101.651](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex.%5Focc.%5Fcode%5Fsection%5F1101.651)

## 4. Business-model benchmarks: discount/flat-fee economics, willingness to pay, CAC, trust in AI

### Takeaway
- **Discount brokerage is a hard volume game.** Redfin is the scale benchmark. It charges sellers 1-1.5%, closed about 50k sides in 2025, lost money for 18 straight quarters, and was absorbed by Rocket (closed July 2025, about $1.8B).
- **Consumers resist paying buyer agents directly.** Only about 20% say they would pay their agent out of pocket, and about 34% (2024) would accept a flat fee.
- **CAC is high.** Portal leads cost about $20-$220+ each, convert at 1-3%, and imply roughly $7k per closing. Zillow Flex referral fees take 15-40% of the gross commission (often 35%).
- **Trust in AI is low and falling.** Trust in AI to help find a home fell from 30% to 16% (Cotality 2026). Buyers want humans in the loop: 68% double-check AI, and 44% would pay for human verification.
- **The positioning these numbers support** is "AI-powered human negotiator" or "AI co-pilot for licensed agents," not a "fully autonomous agent."

## Inferences (my reasoning, not sourced)

1. **Market size.** 4.06M sales × about $414k median gives about $1.7T of transaction value per year. The median understates the mean, so the true figure is probably higher. At about 5.4% combined commission, the pool is roughly $90B per year, with buyer-side commissions around $40B. Texas is about 335k sales × $335k ≈ $112B, or about $6B in commissions. The Austin metro is roughly 25-30k closings per year, extrapolated from about 2.2k in Sept 2026, which is unverified. Its commission pool is then about $0.5-0.7B.
2. **What the product could be worth.** In Austin, concessions and repair credits run about $5-15k per deal. A better negotiator that wins even $2-5k more per side, or saves a deal from cancelling, creates value equal to a large share of a flat fee. That supports a success-fee or flat-fee price of $1-5k, but only if it is delivered through a licensed entity.
3. **The best wedge is the option-period renegotiation.** It is a defined, document-driven step (inspection report, repair amendment, credit), it is the top cancellation cause, and Texas's unrestricted termination right makes the stakes concrete. It is a more credible MVP than "AI negotiates the whole deal."
4. **Regulation forces a licensed model.** In Texas the path is a licensed brokerage, or software sold to licensees. A pure-SaaS company negotiating for consumers would be practicing unlicensed brokerage. Two AI agents from one company representing both sides are an intermediary arrangement, which brings consent requirements and conflict optics that may scare consumers. Selling to two different brokerages, one per side, avoids that.
5. **The founder's "agents are zero-sum" claim isn't tested here.** Nothing above measures realtor negotiation quality. The persistence of 2.4% buyer-side commissions does show consumers still pay for human representation, so an AI product would compete with incumbents on outcome, not just price.
6. **Distribution is the hard part.** A $7k CAC per closing against a $1-5k fee doesn't work. Distribution likely has to come through brokerages, agent teams, or embedded partners such as lenders and inspectors, not direct-to-consumer portal leads.
